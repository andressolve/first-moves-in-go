Lesson 7 of 8

Sente and Gote: the Initiative

In every game of Go, one question comes up on almost every move: who gets to choose where the next important move is played? The player who keeps choosing usually wins. This lesson teaches you to tell which moves force an answer and which don’t, when you can safely ignore a threat, and how to finish a fight so that the next big move is yours.

  • Tell whether a move is sente (it must be answered) or gote (it can be ignored).
  • Play a forcing move first and the big move second, and choose the version of a sequence that leaves you to move.
  • Decide when to ignore a threat and play elsewhere, by comparing what you lose with what you gain.
  • Keep forcing moves that gain nothing in reserve, for example as ko threats.

Part 1

Who chooses the next move?

Some moves demand an answer. If you put your opponent’s stones in atari, your opponent usually has to save them. When they do, it is your turn again, and you are free to play anywhere you like. Other moves demand nothing: your opponent can ignore them and play the biggest move somewhere else.

Go players have a name for each kind of move. A move that your opponent must answer is sente (先手), which means “first hand”. A move that your opponent does not need to answer is gote (後手), which means “second hand”. Step through the example below to see the difference.

Sente and gote

A move is sente if your opponent must answer it, so that you get to move again afterwards. A move is gote if your opponent can ignore it and play elsewhere.

Players also say that you have sente when it is your turn and nothing on the board needs answering. Then you choose where the next important move is played. Keeping sente is how you get the big points before your opponent does.

If you play chess

Sente is what chess players call a tempo. A check, an attack on a piece or a threat of mate forces a reply, and if your threatening move also improves your position, you have gained a tempo. That instinct is a big help in Go: look for moves that threaten something and build something at the same time.

One chess instinct misleads you, though: the feeling that every threat must be answered. In chess you must answer a check by the rules, and losing a piece is usually a disaster. In Go nothing is compulsory, and losing a stone or two is often a small price. A player who answers every threat lets the opponent decide where the whole game is played. Part 5 shows you when to ignore a threat.

Part 2

Must you answer?

To tell whether your opponent’s move is sente, ask yourself one question: if I don’t answer, what will my opponent play next, and what will it cost me? These are the most common kinds of sente move:

  • An atari. If you don’t save the stones, they are captured.
  • A threat to cut. If you don’t connect, your opponent plays between your stones and splits them into weak groups that may be captured.
  • A threat to break into territory. A stone on the border of your territory may threaten to push further in, so that you lose points.

If the honest answer is “nothing much”, the move is gote, and you are free to play the biggest move elsewhere. Try it on two positions. In both, Black has just played the stone with the small circle on it.

Part 3

Free moves and expensive moves

Look back at the example in Part 1. Black’s atari at E3 gained something: Black’s wall moved one point further into the bottom of the board. And because White had to answer, the gain cost Black nothing. Black still got the corner at G7 on the very next move.

A gote move is different. After it, your opponent plays the biggest move on the board. So a gote move is a trade: you get whatever your move gains, and your opponent gets the best point that is left.

The price of a move

A sente move that gains even a little is free: you keep the gain and you still get to choose the next move.

A gote move costs you the initiative. Play it only if it is worth more than the move your opponent will play in reply.

Part 4

Ending a fight in sente

Fights in one part of the board are usually short sequences: you play, your opponent answers, and sometimes you have to answer back. What matters is who plays the last move of the sequence. If your opponent has to play it, you end in sente and you choose the next move. If you have to play it, you end in gote, and your opponent chooses.

Often you can choose between two versions of a sequence that look almost the same. Here is the position from Part 1 again. This time Black puts the white stone in atari from the other side.

Both ataris threatened the same stone. The difference was what happened to Black’s own stone. At E3 it was safely connected, so White’s answer ended the sequence. At F4 it was weak, so Black had to spend another move on it. Before you play a move that forces an answer, check that the answer won’t create a new threat against you.

Part 5

Tenuki: when to ignore a threat

Playing somewhere else instead of answering your opponent’s last move is called tenuki (手抜き). Sente is not a rule of the game. It is a judgement, and your opponent can always choose to ignore your move.

Before you answer a threat, compare two things:

  • What you lose if you ignore it and your opponent carries out the threat.
  • What you gain by playing the biggest move somewhere else instead.

If the move elsewhere is bigger, tenuki is right. Then your opponent faces the same question: carry out the threat, or answer your move?

If you play Civilization or Polytopia

In both games the turn is your scarcest resource. A player who sends units to every tile the AI raids never finishes the wonder or the next city. A player who ignores an army marching on the capital loses the game. Go asks you to make this judgement on every single move.

The instinct that misleads is letting the opponent’s last move decide where you play. Against a computer in Civilization or Polytopia, it is easy to spend whole turns reacting to whatever the AI just did and forget your own plan. In Go, answer only the threats that are bigger than the best move of your own.

Part 6

Keep forcing moves in reserve

A sente move that you play mainly so that your opponent has to answer is called a forcing move (利かし kikashi). Black’s atari at E3 in Part 1 was a forcing move.

Once you know about forcing moves, it is tempting to play every one as soon as you see it, since your opponent has to answer anyway. That is often a mistake. While you haven’t played a forcing move, the threat stays on the board, and your opponent has to keep it in mind. Once you play it and your opponent answers, the weakness is mended for good. A forcing move that gains nothing right now just throws away one of your options.

Go players call the possibilities hidden in a position aji (味), which means “taste”. If White’s shape has weaknesses that Black might use later, players say White has bad aji. Strong players leave aji alone until they can use it.

One of the best uses of a forcing move you have saved is as a ko threat. You met ko threats in Lesson 3: a move elsewhere that your opponent must answer, so that you may retake the ko. Here is the position from Part 1 again, with one difference: there is a ko in the upper right, and Black has not played the atari at E3 yet.

So when should you play a forcing move? Play it when it gains something, as Black’s atari at E3 did in Part 1. Play it when your opponent is about to remove it, for example by playing the same point. And play it when you need it as a ko threat. Otherwise, keep it.

Part 7

Problems: forcing move first

In every problem you play Black, and you make two moves. First play a forcing move. The computer plays White’s answer. Then play your second move. If you get a move wrong, the board resets and you start the problem again.

Part 8

Play a full game and keep sente

Play a 9×9 game against the computer and use what you learned. Some advice before you start:

  • Ask before every move. Does the computer’s last move threaten anything? If it does, how much would you lose, and is that more than the best move elsewhere?
  • Don’t answer moves that threaten nothing. If nothing is in danger, take the biggest point on the board instead.
  • Look for moves that do two jobs. The best moves often defend one of your weaknesses and threaten something at the same time, so your opponent must answer and you keep sente.
  • Finish fights in sente. Before a forcing move, check that your own stones will be safe after your opponent answers, so that you don’t have to spend another move on them.
  • Keep forcing moves that gain nothing. Save them for later. If a ko starts, they are your ko threats.
  • Use the hint. The Who owns what? button shows who the computer thinks will own each point. Large areas that nobody owns yet are where the big moves are.

When both sides have passed, check the scoring screen and press Accept the result. If the computer is too strong at first, choose Komi 0.5.

Summary

What to remember

  1. A sente move must be answered, so you get to move again. A gote move can be ignored, so your opponent chooses the next move.
  2. To judge a move, ask: if I don’t answer, what will my opponent do next, and what will it cost me?
  3. A sente move that gains even a little is free. A gote move must be worth more than the move your opponent plays in reply.
  4. Choose the version of a sequence that ends with your opponent answering, so that you end in sente.
  5. Tenuki is right when the move elsewhere is bigger than what you lose by ignoring the threat.
  6. Keep forcing moves that gain nothing in reserve. Play them when they gain something, when your opponent is about to remove them, or as ko threats.

Words from this lesson

Sente (先手)
A move that your opponent must answer, so that you move again. “Having sente” means it is your turn and nothing needs answering.
Gote (後手)
A move that your opponent can ignore, so that they choose where to play next.
Tenuki (手抜き)
Playing elsewhere instead of answering your opponent’s last move.
