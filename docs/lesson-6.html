Lesson 6 of 8

Cutting and Connecting

Most fights in Go start with one question: are these stones connected, or can they be split apart? A player who keeps their stones together and splits the opponent’s stones apart usually wins the fight, often without capturing anything at first. This lesson teaches you to see the links between stones, to protect your own and to break your opponent’s.

  • Explain why one connected group is stronger than two separate ones.
  • Protect a weak point with a solid connection or a hanging connection.
  • Find every cutting point in a position, yours and your opponent’s.
  • Cut when the cut wins something, and know when a cut will backfire.

Part 1

Why connected stones are strong

Stones that touch along a line form one chain, and a chain shares all of its liberties. A chain of three stones in the open might have seven or eight liberties. Three single stones scattered around have only a few liberties each, and each one has to be defended separately.

Playing a stone that splits your opponent’s stones apart is called a cut (切り kiri). The empty point where a cut can be played is a cutting point. A cut does not capture anything by itself. What it does is turn one group into two, and two weak groups are much harder to look after than one strong group, because you can only play one move at a time.

Step through the example. Black’s stones at C3 and D4 touch only at a corner. White already has a stone at D3, so the only point that still links them is C4.

The idea of this lesson

One strong group is better than two weak groups. When your opponent has two weak groups, you can threaten both of them, and they can save only one with each move. That is why players fight so hard over the points that join or split groups.

If you play chess

You already know that pieces which defend each other are hard to win, and that a loose, undefended piece is a target. A cut in Go is like a move that breaks the chain of protection between two pieces. The instinct that helps: look for undefended targets. The instinct that misleads: in chess, a defended piece is still a separate piece. In Go, stones that connect become a single unit with shared liberties, so connecting does more than protect. It makes one stronger thing out of two weaker ones.

If you play Civilization or Polytopia

Think of an army that has been cut off from its cities. It cannot be reinforced, it cannot retreat, and it has to survive on its own. A cut-off group of stones is in the same position: it must either make two eyes by itself or run until it reaches friendly stones. The instinct that misleads is spreading out to grab more land. Spreading out is good in Go too, but every gap you leave is a place where your opponent might cut your empire into pieces.

Part 2

Two ways to connect

When your opponent threatens to cut, you can protect the cutting point in two ways.

  • The solid connection (堅ツギ katatsugi) fills the cutting point with your own stone. The stones now touch along lines and form one chain. It can never be cut, but it is slow, because it adds a stone right next to stones you already have.
  • The hanging connection (カケツギ kaketsugi) leaves the cutting point empty and puts a stone one point away, so that three of your stones surround the cutting point. If your opponent plays into that point, their stone has only one liberty, and you capture it on your next move. This shape is called a tiger’s mouth (虎の口 tora no kuchi): a stone that walks in gets eaten.

In the example, Black has already made a tiger’s mouth around the point D3. Watch what happens when White tries to cut there.

The hanging connection is often better than the solid one, because it reaches further and claims more of the board. But it has one weakness to check every time: the fourth side of the tiger’s mouth. A tiger’s mouth works only if a stone played inside it would be in atari. If your opponent already has a stone touching the inside point, a stone played there joins that stone, gets more liberties and cuts you after all.

Your turn: connect against a threat

You are Black in both problems. Each time, White is threatening to cut at D3. Play one move that stops the cut.

Part 3

Shapes that hold and shapes that can be cut

You don’t have to connect every pair of stones solidly. Some shapes are already safe, because whenever your opponent tries to cut, you have an answer. The key idea is that the shape has two ways to connect. If your opponent takes one of them, you take the other. Two points that work like this, where taking one lets the other player take the other, are called miai (見合い).

  • The diagonal connection: two stones that touch at a corner. There are two empty points between them. If White plays on one, Black plays on the other.
  • The bamboo joint (竹フ takefu): two pairs of stones side by side with a gap between them. Again there are two points that link them, and they are miai.

Faster shapes, where your stones are further apart, cover more of the board but can be cut. The one-point jump (two stones on the same line with one empty point between them) and the knight’s move (two stones placed like a knight’s move in chess) can both be broken by a stone that pushes into the gap. Pushing a stone into a gap between two enemy stones is called a wedge.

So are the jump and the knight’s move bad? Not at all. The wedging stone always lands between two enemy stones with few liberties, so it is weak too. If you have even one more stone nearby, the wedge usually gets captured. Strong players use these fast shapes when the area is already friendly, and switch to solid shapes when the opponent is strong nearby.

Part 4

Finding cutting points

Before every move in a real game, strong players glance over the board for cutting points, in their own stones and in their opponent’s. Here is a simple way to find them.

  1. Look for two of your stones that touch only at a corner (diagonally).
  2. Look at the two empty points between them. If both are empty, the stones are safe: the two points are miai.
  3. If your opponent already has a stone on one of the two points, the other point is a cutting point, unless a stone played there would be in atari straight away, as in a tiger’s mouth.

Part 5

Cut, then capture

A cut is at its strongest when it leaves one of the separated stones short of liberties. Then the cut is the first move of a capture: you cut, and the cut-off stone falls into a ladder or a net, or into a double atari.

To find these cuts, look at your opponent’s stones the way you looked at your own in Part 4. Find a diagonal pair where you already hold one of the two points between them. Then ask: if I cut at the other point, how many liberties will each of the separated stones have?

Your turn: cut and capture

You are Black in all three problems. In the first two, capture the marked white stone. If White runs, the computer answers for White, and you keep going until the stone is captured. In the third, find one move that puts two white stones in atari at once.

Part 6

When a cut backfires, and the crosscut

A cut also creates something new: your cutting stone. It sits between enemy stones, and it is often short of liberties. Before you cut, ask the same question you asked about your opponent’s stones: how many liberties will my cutting stone have, and can my opponent capture it? If the cutting stone can be captured, the cut has given your opponent a free target, and the two stones you tried to separate end up stronger than before.

In the next example, Black cuts without asking that question.

When a cut is answered by a cut, four stones end up meeting in a square, two of each colour, each pair touching only at the corner. This is called a crosscut (切り違い kirichigai). Each player’s stones are cut apart, and every one of the four stones is weak.

A Go proverb

“When in doubt, extend from a crosscut.” In a crosscut, the tempting move is an atari. But an atari usually just makes your opponent extend, and leaves you with one more weak stone to look after. Extending one of your own crosscut stones, so that it becomes a chain of two with more liberties, is usually stronger. Extend the stone that is in more danger, and extend away from the opponent’s strength.

Part 7

Play a full game

Play a complete 9×9 game against the computer. This time, pay special attention to cuts and connections:

  • Check your own cutting points after every computer move. Look for pairs of your stones that touch only at a corner where the computer already holds one of the two points between them.
  • Connect when the cut would hurt. If a cut would leave one of your stones short of liberties, connect first. A hanging connection is often better than a solid one, but check that the fourth side of the tiger’s mouth is not touched by a computer stone.
  • Look for the computer’s cutting points too. Before you cut, count the liberties of the stones you would separate and of your own cutting stone. Cut when the cut wins something; don’t cut if your cutting stone would be captured.
  • Don’t let your stones split into two weak groups. If you have to choose, keep one group strong rather than two groups half-safe.
  • In a crosscut, extend. Make the weaker of your two crosscut stones into a chain with more liberties, instead of giving an atari.

Finish one game, win or lose, to complete the lesson. If the Beginner computer is too strong at first, choose Komi 0.5 for an easier game.

Summary

What to remember

  1. One strong group is better than two weak groups. A cut turns one group into two, and your opponent can save only one of them at a time.
  2. You can protect a cutting point with a solid connection, which fills the point, or a hanging connection, which makes a tiger’s mouth around it.
  3. A tiger’s mouth works only if a stone played inside it would be in atari. Check the fourth side.
  4. The diagonal connection and the bamboo joint cannot be cut, because they have two ways to connect (miai). The one-point jump and the knight’s move can be cut by a wedge, but the wedging stone is weak too.
  5. A cutting point is the second point between two diagonal stones when the opponent already holds the first.
  6. Cut when the cut leaves enemy stones short of liberties. Don’t cut when your own cutting stone would be captured.
  7. In a crosscut, extend the stone in more danger rather than giving an atari.

Words from this lesson

Cut
A move that separates enemy stones that could otherwise have been joined.
Cutting point
An empty point where a stone would cut two enemy stones apart.
Solid connection
Filling a cutting point with your own stone, so that the stones form one chain.
Hanging connection
Protecting a cutting point by surrounding it on three sides, so that an enemy stone played there would be in atari.
Tiger’s mouth
The shape of three stones around an empty point, made by a hanging connection.
Miai
Two points that work as a pair: if your opponent takes one, you take the other.
Diagonal connection
Two stones that touch at a corner, with two empty points between them that are miai.
Bamboo joint
Two pairs of stones side by side with a one-point gap. It cannot be cut.
One-point jump
Two stones on the same line with one empty point between them.
Knight’s move
Two stones placed like a chess knight’s move: two points along and one across.
Wedge
A stone pushed into the gap between two enemy stones.
Crosscut
Four stones in a square, two of each colour, with each colour’s pair touching only at the corner.
