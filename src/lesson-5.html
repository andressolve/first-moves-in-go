Lesson 5 of 8

The Opening

In Lesson 4 you played your first full game, and you probably wondered where to put your first few stones. This lesson answers that question. Unlike chess, Go has no opening book you need to memorise. A handful of principles tell you where the big points are, and on a 9×9 board a good or bad opening can decide the whole game.

  • Choose a good first move in a corner or at the centre, and say what it gains and what it gives up.
  • Explain the difference between low moves, which take territory, and high moves, which build influence.
  • Enclose your own corner, approach your opponent’s, and extend along a side by the right distance.
  • Recognise four common opening mistakes, and find the biggest point on the board.

Part 1

What the opening is for

The opening (布石 fuseki) is the first part of the game, while the board is still mostly empty. Nobody is captured yet. Instead, both players stake out the areas they hope to turn into territory later, a little like explorers planting flags on an unmapped continent.

Because a stone never moves once it is played, every opening move is a long-term investment. A good opening move does three things: it claims a lot of land, it works together with your other stones, and it makes it harder for your opponent to claim land of their own.

In Lesson 4 you saw that six stones enclose 9 points in a corner, 4 on the edge and only 2 in the centre. That is why the traditional order of the opening is corners first, then the sides, then the centre. Step through this example to see the order in a real-looking 9×9 opening.

The order of the opening

Claim the corners first, then the sides, then the centre. A 9×9 board has one exception: the centre point is so close to all four corners that it is often worth playing very early, even as the first move. You will see why in Part 3.

If you play chess

Chess players memorise opening lines because one inaccurate move can lose a pawn or walk into a fast mate. A Go opening move is almost never a blunder like that. It is better or worse by a few points, so instead of memorising sequences you learn to ask one question: which move claims the most and leaves my opponent the least?

Some chess habits carry over well. “Develop every piece” becomes “give every stone a job”. “Don’t move the same piece twice in the opening” becomes “don’t add stones to a group that is already safe”. “Control the centre” is good advice on a 9×9 board, though less so on the big 19×19 board. One chess habit misleads: chasing a piece early to gain time. In chess, a harassed piece has to move. In Go, a lone stone in open space cannot be captured, and chasing it usually just helps it grow stronger.

Part 2

Low and high: the lines of the board

Go players describe a point by how far it is from the nearest edge. The edge itself is the first line, the row just inside it is the second line, and so on. On a 9×9 board there are only five lines: the fifth line runs through the centre point, E5. Step through the example to see what a stone does on each line.

A stone on the third line or lower is called low. A stone on the fourth line or higher is called high. Low stones take land now. High stones build influence: strength that faces the centre and helps you in later fights. Influence is not territory yet, but it can turn into territory, or help you attack your opponent’s stones, later in the game.

Low and high

The third line is the line of territory. The fourth line is the line of influence. A good opening mixes the two, depending on what you need. The first and second lines are almost always too low early in the game, because a stone there claims only a thin strip along the edge.

The corner points

Most opening moves are made on a few special points near the corners. The point on the third line from both edges is called the 3-3 point (三々 san-san). On a 9×9 board, the four 3-3 points are C3, G3, C7 and G7: they are the dots printed on the board, apart from the one in the centre.

A point on the third line from one edge and the fourth line from the other edge is called a 3-4 point (小目 komoku). Each corner has two of them. In the lower-left corner they are C4 (third line from the left edge, fourth line from the bottom edge) and D3 (fourth line from the left edge, third line from the bottom edge).

Part 3

The first move: corner or centre?

On a 9×9 board, three first moves are common: a 3-3 point, a 3-4 point, and the centre point. Each one makes a different trade between security and influence. None of them is a mistake. Step through the example to compare them.

The centre point has its own name: tengen (天元), which means “origin of heaven”. On a big 19×19 board, tengen is far from every corner and is rarely played first. On a 9×9 board, it is only two lines away from every 3-3 point in both directions, so it helps in all four corners at once. Strong players and computer programs often start 9×9 games there.

You may also read about the 4-4 point, which is the most popular first move on 19×19 boards. On a 9×9 board the 4-4 point is D4: so close to the centre that it behaves more like a centre move than a corner move, and it leaves the 3-3 point underneath it open for your opponent. It is less common on 9×9 than the other three choices.

If you play Civilization or Polytopia

Choosing your first move is like founding your first city. A city tucked into a corner of the map, with coast on two sides, is safe and quick to develop: that is the 3-3 point. A city in the middle of the map can expand in every direction, but it has no natural defences: that is tengen.

The instinct that helps is expanding fast: claim the good sites before your opponent does, and don’t spend early turns on things that can wait. The instinct that misleads is the early rush. In Polytopia, a quick attack on a nearby city can win the game. In Go, attacking a stone at the start of the game rarely works, because there is so much empty space around it that it can always run or settle.

Part 4

Enclosing and approaching a corner

A stone on a 3-4 point claims a corner, but not completely. One side of the corner is still open. If you add a second stone that closes the open side, the two stones form an enclosure (締まり shimari), and the corner is yours. If your opponent plays near your corner stone first, so that you can no longer enclose it, that move is called an approach (掛かり kakari).

Which is better, enclosing your own corner or approaching your opponent’s? Both moves are big, and strong players often disagree. This is a judgement call, not a calculation. What matters most is the direction of the approach: approach from the side where you already have stones, so that your approaching stone has a friend nearby and is hard to attack.

Try both ideas. In problem 1 you play Black and enclose your corner. In problem 2 you play White and approach Black’s corner. Each problem has two good answers; find either of them.

Part 5

Extending along the side

After the corners, the next big points are on the sides. An extension (開き hiraki) is a move along the side, usually on the third line, a few points away from your other stones. It claims the land in between, and it gives your group room along the edge to make two eyes if it is ever attacked. A group with that kind of room is said to have a base.

The hard part is the distance. If you extend too little, your stones are safe but claim very little. If you extend too far, your opponent can play in the gap, and instead of one strong group you have two weak ones. Players use a rule of thumb for this:

How far to extend

From a single stone on the third line, extend two spaces. From a wall two stones tall, extend three spaces. From a wall three stones tall, extend four spaces. The stronger the stones behind you, the further you may go, because a strong wall helps you attack anything that lands in the gap.

The rule is easiest to see on a bigger board, so the next example uses a 13×13 board. A “space” is an empty point left between your stones along the line.

On a 9×9 board, each side has only five points on the third line between the two 3-3 points, so the distances shrink. An extension from one corner often reaches all the way to the next corner. The idea is the same, though: extend as far as your stones can safely defend, and no further.

Part 6

Four common opening mistakes

Mistake 1: crawling along the second line

When an opponent’s stones press down on yours from above, it is tempting to keep pushing along underneath them, gaining one point at a time. Players call this crawling. On the second line it is almost always a bad trade, as the example shows.

An old proverb puts it in three lines: the second line is the line of defeat, the third line is the line of territory, and the fourth line is the line of influence. If you are being pushed along the second line, stop crawling and play somewhere big, or jump out toward the centre before it is too late.

Mistake 2: overconcentration

A strong group doesn’t need more stones. Adding stones right next to a group that is already safe is called overconcentration (凝り形 korigatachi): too many stones in one place, doing work that one stone could do. The best way to use a strong wall is to play some distance away from it, so that the wall and the new stone together claim a large area.

Mistake 3: touching your opponent’s stones too early

When you play a stone directly next to an enemy stone, your opponent usually answers by adding a stone next to yours. Each of those answers makes their group stronger, and your stone, with its few liberties, is the one that ends up under pressure. A Go proverb says: don’t touch the stones you want to attack. In the opening, approach from a distance, as you did in Part 4. Contact moves are for fights that have already started, or for defending your own stones.

If you play chess

In chess, attacking a piece with a pawn gains time, because the piece has to move away. In Go, nothing moves. A stone that you touch simply gets a reinforcement next to it on your opponent’s next turn. Touching an enemy stone is how you help it become strong, not how you hurt it.

Mistake 4: ignoring a big point

The most common mistake of all is playing a move that is good but small while a much bigger point is still empty. Before every opening move, look over the whole board and ask: is there still an empty corner? Has anyone taken the centre? Is there a side where neither player has a stone? Small local moves, such as blocking one point on the edge, can almost always wait. The next part is practice at exactly this.

Part 8

Play a game, and watch your opening

Play a complete 9×9 game against the computer. This time, pay special attention to your first eight or ten moves. Some advice:

  • Start with a big point. As Black, open at the centre point E5, at a 3-3 point or at a 3-4 point. As White, take a corner the computer hasn’t touched.
  • Count the empty corners. Before each of your first few moves, check whether any corner is still empty. If one is, it is probably the biggest point on the board.
  • Stay on the third and fourth lines. Early in the game, a move on the first or second line claims almost nothing.
  • Don’t crowd your own stones. If one of your groups is already safe, play somewhere else. Leave space between your stones, as in an extension.
  • Don’t touch the computer’s stones too early. Approach from a distance, from the side where you already have stones.
  • Check your opening. After about ten moves, press Who owns what? to see how the computer thinks the board is divided. If most of the board is already shaded in the computer’s colour, think about which of your moves were too low, too close together or too small.

The game ends and is scored exactly as in Lesson 4. Finish one game, win or lose, to complete the lesson.

Summary

What to remember

  1. The opening is about claiming land efficiently. Every move should claim a lot, work with your other stones and make your opponent’s job harder.
  2. Corners first, then sides, then the centre. On a 9×9 board, the centre point is also big from the very start, because it is close to every corner.
  3. The third line is the line of territory, the fourth line is the line of influence, and the second line is the line of defeat.
  4. A 3-3 stone secures a corner on its own. A 3-4 stone needs a second stone, an enclosure, to secure it, and your opponent may approach it first. Approach from the side where you already have stones.
  5. Extend two spaces from a single stone, three from a wall two stones tall and four from a wall three stones tall.
  6. Avoid crawling on the second line, adding stones to groups that are already safe, touching enemy stones too early and playing small moves while a big point is still empty.
