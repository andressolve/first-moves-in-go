Lesson 1 of 8

Liberties and Capture

Go has almost no rules, and a game between strong players is still one of the hardest things humans do. This lesson covers the one rule that everything else is built on: how stones stay alive, and how they die.

  • Place stones and count their liberties.
  • Capture a stone or a whole chain of stones.
  • Spot a stone in atari and save it.
  • Beat the computer at Capture Go.

Part 1

The game in one paragraph

Two players, Black and White, take turns placing one stone on the board. Black moves first. Stones are placed on the intersections, where the lines cross, not inside the squares. Once a stone is placed, it never moves. At the end of the game, the player who controls more of the board wins.

That last sentence is the real goal: Go is a game about territory. But before you can understand territory, you need to know what makes stones safe and what gets them captured. That is what the first three lessons are about.

Part 2

Liberties: the air a stone breathes

A stone’s liberties are the empty points right next to it, directly along the lines: up, down, left and right. A stone in the open has four liberties.

Diagonal points are not liberties. The lines don’t connect diagonally, so neither do stones.

The edge of the board works like a wall. A stone on the edge has lost one direction, and a stone in the corner has lost two.

Part 3

Capture: take away every liberty

When a stone has no liberties left, it is captured: it is taken off the board, and the player who captured it keeps it as a prisoner. You capture by placing your stones on all of the enemy stone’s liberties.

Step through the example below. Watch the green dots: they are White’s remaining liberties.

Atari

A stone with only one liberty left is in atari (当たり). It can be captured on the very next move. Saying “atari” in Go is a bit like saying “check” in chess: it warns that something is about to be taken.

If you play chess

There is one big difference from check. In chess you must get out of check. In Go you are allowed to ignore atari and play somewhere else, and good players sometimes do. If the stone in danger isn’t worth much, saving it is a waste of a move.

Your turn: capture in one move

In each problem, you are Black. Find the move that captures. Use the numbered buttons to jump between problems.

Part 4

Chains share their liberties

Stones of the same colour that touch along a line are connected. They form a chain (some books say group or string), and a chain lives or dies as one unit. Its liberties are all the empty points next to any of its stones, each counted once.

This is why connecting your stones makes them stronger: more stones usually means more liberties, and the enemy needs more moves to fill them all.

Now the question that trips up almost every beginner. Read it carefully before you answer.

Try it yourself on the board below. Play stones for both sides and press Count liberties to see each chain’s liberty count written on one of its stones. Connect stones and watch the numbers grow; surround a chain and watch its number shrink.

Part 5

Escaping from atari

If your stone is in atari, you usually have two ways to save it:

  1. Run. Add a stone on its last liberty. The new stone joins the chain and brings its own liberties with it.
  2. Capture an attacker. If one of the enemy stones surrounding you is itself short of liberties, capture it. That opens up space around your stone.

Your turn: save the white stone

Now you are White. Each problem has exactly one move that saves the stone marked with a triangle.

Part 6

Play Capture Go

Capture Go is a short game that teachers use to train exactly what you just learned. The rules are normal Go rules with one change: whoever captures a stone first wins.

Play on the small 7×7 board below against the computer. Some advice before you start:

  • After every move, ask two questions: “Which of my chains has the fewest liberties?” and “Which of the computer’s chains has the fewest?”
  • Keep your stones connected. Loose single stones are easy targets.
  • A chain with two liberties is in danger: one more enemy move puts it in atari.
  • The danger rings help while you learn: a red ring means atari (one liberty), and a dashed yellow ring means two liberties. Turn them off when you feel ready.

Start on Normal. When you can beat it twice in a row, try Hard.

Summary

What to remember

  1. A stone’s liberties are the empty points directly next to it along the lines. Diagonals don’t count.
  2. Stones touching along a line form one chain, and the chain shares all its liberties.
  3. A chain with no liberties left is captured. A chain with one liberty left is in atari.
  4. The edge and the corner take liberties away, so stones there are easier to capture.
  5. To save a chain in atari, either extend it into open space or capture one of the stones attacking it.
