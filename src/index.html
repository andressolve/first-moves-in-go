First Moves in Go

Go is four thousand years old, has rules you can learn in ten minutes, and was the last classic board game that computers learned to play at a world-class level. These lessons teach you to play it, one idea at a time, on boards you can click.

The lessons

  • 1Liberties and CaptureHow stones stay alive and how they die. Ends with a game of Capture Go against the computer.
  • 2Ladders and NetsReading ahead: chasing a stone across the board, and knowing before you start whether the chase will work.
  • 3Suicide and KoThe two rules that look strange at first, why Go needs them, and the tricks they make possible.
  • 4Life and TerritoryTwo eyes, the end of the game, and counting the score. Ends with a full 9×9 game against the computer.
  • 5The OpeningWhere to put your first stones: corners, sides and the centre, low moves versus high moves, and the mistakes that lose a game early.
  • 6Cutting and ConnectingKeeping your stones together and splitting your opponent’s apart: connections, cutting points and the crosscut.
  • 7Sente and GoteWhich moves your opponent must answer and which they can ignore, when to play elsewhere, and how to finish a fight so that the next big move is yours.
  • 8Life-and-Death ShapesWhich eye spaces live, which die, and which depend on who plays first: vital points, false eyes, seki, and eight problems to test them.

Want to play each other? Play a Friend is a board for two players on one screen, on 9×9, 13×13 or 19×19, with full rules and scoring.

How to use these lessons

Do the lessons in order, because each one builds on the last. Every lesson mixes short explanations with boards you can play on, puzzles that tell you why a wrong answer fails, and a game against the computer at the end. A lesson takes about 30 to 45 minutes. The bar at the top of each lesson shows how many of its exercises you have finished; your progress is saved in this browser.

If you get a puzzle wrong, read the explanation before trying again. In Go, as in chess, understanding why a move fails teaches more than finding the right move by luck.
