Lesson 4 of 8

Life and Territory

So far you have been fighting over stones. Now the real game begins. This lesson shows why some groups can never be captured, how those groups claim land, and how to count who won. By the end you will play and score a complete game on a 9×9 board.

  • Tell whether a group is alive or dead by counting its eyes.
  • Make a second eye, or stop your opponent from making one.
  • Explain why the corners are the best land to claim first.
  • End a game, count it with area scoring and play a full game.

Part 1

Back to the real goal: territory

In Lesson 1 you read that the player who controls more of the board wins. Here is what “control” means. Your territory is the empty points that are completely surrounded by your stones, so that no enemy stone can live there. At the end of the game, every point you surround counts for you.

Capturing is still important, but only as a means to an end. A capture is useful when it wins you land or saves your own stones. Capturing a stone that doesn’t matter, while your opponent fences off a whole corner, is a bad trade.

If you play Civilization or Polytopia

Think of territory as your borders. A city’s borders count for you only if the enemy can’t walk in and settle there. In Go, a wall of stones does the same job, and the score at the end is simply how much land sits inside your borders.

Part 2

Two eyes: the secret of life

If a group of stones is completely surrounded, can it always be captured? Not always. Everything depends on the empty points inside the group, which are called eyes. An eye is an empty point (or a small empty area) that is surrounded by one player’s stones.

First, a group with only one eye. Black fills the outside liberties one at a time. The last liberty is inside the eye, and Black is allowed to play there because that stone captures the whole group.

Now a group with two separate eyes. Black fills every outside liberty, just as before. But then Black is stuck: a stone in either eye would have no liberties, and it would capture nothing, because the other eye still gives White a liberty. From Lesson 3 you know that such a move is suicide, and suicide is not allowed.

The rule of life

A group with two separate eyes can never be captured, no matter how many moves the opponent makes. Such a group is alive. A surrounded group that cannot make two eyes is dead: it will be captured eventually, so it counts as captured at the end of the game.

Check it for yourself. On the board below you play Black. The white group has two eyes, at A1 and C1. Try to capture it, and see what happens when you play inside an eye.

Alive or dead?

In both questions, White has no way to escape outward. Black is to play. Decide whether White’s group lives.

Part 3

Life and death: the vital point

A surrounded group often has a small empty space that could become two eyes or only one, depending on who plays first. Inside that space there is usually a single key point, called the vital point. If the defender takes it, the space splits into two eyes. If the attacker takes it, the space can only ever make one eye.

This is where the reading you practised with ladders pays off. Before you play, work out what happens after each move, just as you would calculate a forcing line in chess.

If you play chess

A life-and-death problem is Go’s version of a mate-in-two puzzle. Strong players solve thousands of them, and for the same reason chess players solve tactics: in a real game, you need to see the answer instantly.

In problems 1 and 3 you play White and must make the group live. In problems 2 and 4 you play Black and must kill it. When you attack, the computer answers with White’s best defence, so keep going until the group is captured.

Part 4

Corners first, then edges, then the centre

Territory needs walls, and walls cost moves. The edge of the board is a free wall that you don’t have to build. A corner has two free walls. That makes the corner the cheapest place to claim land.

Step through the example. Each time, Black uses exactly six stones. Count how much land each set of six stones encloses.

If you play Civilization or Polytopia

This is the same idea as choosing a good city site. A city on a coast or against mountains is easier to defend because nature guards one side for you. In Go, the corner is the best site on the map, so strong players almost always start there. On a large 19×19 board, the first four moves of a game are nearly always in the four corners.

Part 5

How a game ends

There is no checkmate in Go. Instead, the game ends when both players agree that nothing useful is left to play.

  • Passing. On your turn, instead of placing a stone, you may pass. When both players pass one after the other, the game is over.
  • When to pass. Pass when every border between your stones and your opponent’s is closed, and every neutral point (explained next) has been filled. At that stage, any move you could make would be either inside your own territory, where it gains nothing, or inside your opponent’s territory, where it would die.
  • Neutral points. An empty point that touches both colours belongs to nobody. These points are called dame (駄目). With the scoring method in this course, filling a neutral point earns you one point, so fill them before you pass.
  • Dead stones. Stones that are surrounded and cannot make two eyes are dead. Nobody needs to spend moves capturing them one by one. At the end, both players agree they are dead and simply take them off the board.

Part 6

Counting the score

This course uses area scoring, the method used in China and in most computer Go. Each player’s score is:

your stones on the board + the empty points that only your stones surround.

Dead stones are removed first, so the points they stood on count for the player who surrounds them. Neutral points count for nobody.

Black has an advantage because Black moves first. To make the game fair, White receives extra points called komi (込み). With area scoring, komi is 7.5 points. The half point means the game can never end in a tie. On a 9×9 board with no neutral points, there are 81 points in total, so Black needs at least 45 of them to beat White plus komi.

Now count two finished games yourself. Type each side’s area into the boxes and press Check. Don’t add komi; the second exercise adds it for you.

The other way to count: territory scoring

In Japan and Korea, and on many online servers, players use territory scoring instead. You count only the empty points you surround, then add the prisoners you captured during the game and the dead stones you removed at the end. Stones on the board don’t count. Komi under these rules is 6.5.

The two methods almost always give the same winner, usually with the same margin or a margin that differs by one point. The one practical difference is that under territory scoring, playing a stone inside your own territory costs you a point, so players stop as soon as the borders are closed. You will meet territory scoring when you play on OGS, and you don’t need to change how you play.

Part 7

Play a full game

You now know every rule of Go. Play a complete game against the computer on a 9×9 board. Some advice before you start:

  • Start in the corners. Your first few stones should claim corners, or the points just inside them, before you build walls along the edges.
  • Don’t over-defend. A territory needs a closed border, not a thick one. Each extra stone inside your own land is a move you didn’t spend claiming more.
  • Use your capturing skills. Look for chains in atari, ladders and nets. A captured group usually turns into territory for the player who captured it.
  • Check your groups for two eyes. If one of your groups is surrounded, make sure it has room for two eyes before you play anywhere else.
  • Pass when the borders are closed. Fill any neutral points first, then press Pass. The computer will pass when it agrees.
  • Ask for a hint. The Who owns what? button shades each point by who the computer thinks will end up owning it. It updates every time the computer moves.

When both sides have passed, the board switches to the scoring screen. Stones the computer believes are dead are shown faded, and small squares show whose area each empty point is. If you disagree about a group, click it to switch it between alive and dead. The table shows both players’ areas and the result. Press Accept the result to finish, or Keep playing if you realise there is still something to fight over.

Start with the Beginner computer. If it is too strong at first, choose Komi 0.5 to give yourself an easier game, and switch back to standard komi once you win.

Summary

What to remember

  1. The goal of Go is territory: the empty points your stones surround.
  2. A group with two separate eyes can never be captured, because filling either eye would be suicide. A surrounded group that can only make one eye is dead.
  3. Inside a small eye space there is usually one vital point. Whoever plays there first decides whether the group lives.
  4. The corner is the cheapest place to make territory, then the edge, then the centre.
  5. The game ends when both players pass. Dead stones are removed, and each side counts stones plus surrounded empty points. White adds 7.5 points of komi.

Words from this lesson

Territory
Empty points surrounded by one player’s stones, where the opponent cannot live.
Eye
An empty point or small empty area surrounded by one player’s stones.
Alive
A group that cannot be captured, usually because it has two separate eyes.
Dead
A group that cannot avoid capture. Dead stones are removed at the end of the game.
Vital point
The key point inside an eye space that decides whether the group can make two eyes.
Dame
A neutral empty point that touches both colours and belongs to nobody.
Pass
Giving up your turn without placing a stone. Two passes in a row end the game.
Area scoring
Counting each player’s stones on the board plus the empty points they surround.
Komi
Extra points given to White to make up for Black moving first: 7.5 with area scoring.

Where to play next

You can now play real games against real people. The free server online-go.com (usually called OGS) lets you play on 9×9 boards against players of your own strength, or against computer opponents at many levels. Start with 9×9 games until you win about half of them, then try 13×13.
