Lesson 8 of 8

Life-and-Death Shapes

In Lesson 4 you learned that a group needs two eyes to live. This lesson teaches you to look at the empty space inside a surrounded group and tell, often at a glance, whether it can make two eyes. A handful of shapes come up again and again. Once you know them, you will stop wasting moves on groups that are already safe, and you will find the one move that kills a group that only looks safe.

  • Tell which eye spaces of three, four, five and six points live, die, or depend on who plays first.
  • Find the vital point of a shape by looking for the point that touches the most others.
  • Recognise a false eye, and make your opponent’s eye false.
  • Shrink an eye space from the outside before you play inside it.
  • Recognise seki, where neither side can attack, and count it correctly.

Part 1

Eye space: the room inside a group

First, a short reminder of Lesson 4. A group that has two separate eyes can never be captured, because the opponent cannot fill either eye without committing suicide. Two empty points next to each other make only one eye, not two. A space of three points in a row, or three points bent around a corner, can become two eyes or only one, depending on who plays first at its middle point, which is called the vital point.

In this lesson we look at the whole empty area inside a surrounded group, which is called its eye space. The size of the eye space matters, and so does its shape. You will see that some shapes are alive however the opponent attacks, some are dead even if the owner plays first, and some are unsettled: they live if the owner plays first and die if the opponent does.

To count an eye space, include every empty point that is surrounded by the group’s stones and the edge of the board. Try it on the board below.

If you play chess

Chess players don’t calculate king and rook against king from scratch every time; they know the technique by heart. The shapes in this lesson are Go’s basic endgames. You will still need to read ahead in your games, but knowing that a shape is alive, dead or unsettled saves you most of the work and tells you where to look.

Part 2

Eye spaces of four points

There are four shapes that four points can make. Two of them are alive, one is dead, and one is unsettled. Every group in this part is completely surrounded by Black, so the eye space is all that White has.

Straight four: alive

Four points in a row along the edge are alive, even if Black plays first. Step through the example to see why.

The two middle points of the straight four work as a pair. If Black takes one of them, White takes the other, and White always ends up with two eyes. Two points that work like this, so that whichever one your opponent takes you can take the other, are called miai (見合い). When a group has miai for its second eye, you don’t need to add a move to it. Save your move and play somewhere else.

Bent four: alive

Square four: dead

Four points in a 2×2 square look roomy, but they can only make one eye. Even if White plays first inside the square, the three points that are left form a bent three, and Black takes its vital point.

Pyramid four: unsettled

The last shape of four points looks like a T. It has one point in the middle that touches all three of the others. Use what you learned about straight three and bent three to answer the question.

Part 3

Five and six points: look for the busiest point

Larger eye spaces are usually alive. Five points in a row, for example, are alive whoever plays first. But a few compact shapes of five and six points are unsettled. They are worth learning, because they appear in real games again and again.

There is a simple way to find the vital point of a compact shape. For each point of the eye space, count how many other points of the space it touches (along the lines, not diagonally). The point that touches the most is usually the vital point. A stone there touches the whole shape, so it stops White from dividing the space into two separate eyes.

How to find the vital point

Count, for every point of the eye space, how many other points of the space it touches. Look first at the point with the highest number. If the owner plays there, the space can usually be split into two eyes. If the opponent plays there, it usually cannot.

Step through the three shapes below. The numbers show how many points of the space each point touches. In each shape Black plays the point with the highest number, and after that White cannot make two eyes, whatever White does next.

The shapes to remember

Dead even if the owner plays first: any space of one or two points, and the square four.

Unsettled, so whoever plays the vital point first decides: straight three, bent three, pyramid four, bulky five and cross five. The flower six (also called rabbitty six) belongs here too: if the attacker plays first, its centre kills.

Alive however the opponent attacks: straight four, bent four on the side, and most spaces of five or more points that are long rather than compact.

Part 4

False eyes

Not every empty point that is surrounded by your stones is a real eye. Sometimes the stones around the eye are not connected to each other, except through the eye itself. The opponent can then put some of those stones in atari, and to save them you must fill the eye. An eye like this is called a false eye.

Step through the example. White seems to have two eyes, at A1 and D1. But look at the white stone on E1. It is connected to the rest of the group only through E2 or through the eye at D1.

The diagonal rule

To check whether an eye is real, look at the points diagonally next to it.

  • In the centre of the board an eye has four diagonal points. If the opponent holds two of them, the eye is false.
  • On the edge an eye has only two diagonal points on the board. If the opponent holds even one, the eye is false.
  • In the corner an eye has one diagonal point. If the opponent holds it, the eye is false.

“Holds” means the opponent has a stone there, or can play there safely whenever they want. So when you attack, playing on an eye’s diagonal point is often the move that kills. When you defend, that same point is often the one you must take first.

Part 5

Make the space smaller first

When an eye space is too big to kill by playing inside it, the attacker starts from the outside. A move that wraps around the end of the opponent’s line of stones, touching the last stone and sitting diagonally next to one of your own stones, is called a hane (ハネ). On the first line, a hane pushes into the eye space and takes a point away from it, and it is safe because it is connected to your own wall. A stone played inside the eye space, away from your own stones, is called a placement (置き oki).

A good habit when you attack: first make the space smaller with hane from the outside, then play the placement on the vital point of what is left. A placement played too early, while the space is still big, usually just gives the defender a target to capture.

If you play Civilization or Polytopia

This is like taking border tiles before attacking a city. A rushed attack on a well-supplied city fails; first you squeeze its territory and cut its supply, and then the city falls with little resistance. In Go, a hane is a cheap, safe move that takes land from the group, and the placement is the final assault once the group no longer has room.

The corner is special

In the corner, a group has the least room of all, because two edges cut its eye space short. In the replay above, Black could also have killed at once with B1, the point on the first line next to the corner. That point is often the vital point of a corner group. Strong players also learn a few famous corner shapes by name, such as the “carpenter’s square”, the same way chess players learn named endgames. You can meet those later, in problem books and on OGS.

Part 6

Seki: when neither side can attack

Sometimes two groups of opposite colours are tangled together, neither one has two eyes, and they share their last liberties. If either player fills a shared liberty, that player’s own group is left in atari, and the opponent captures it. So neither player wants to move there, and both groups stay on the board until the end of the game. This is called seki (セキ), which means mutual life.

In the example below, at the bottom left, a black group of six stones (A2 to E2 and E1) is surrounded by White, and a white group of two stones (B1 and C1) is surrounded by Black. Neither has an eye. Both groups have exactly the same two liberties: A1 and D1.

Seki can also happen when each group has one eye and the two groups share a liberty: neither side can fill the shared liberty without putting its own group in atari. Whatever form it takes, the rule for players is the same: leave a seki alone. Don’t fill a shared liberty yourself, and pass when only shared liberties are left.

How seki is counted. With area scoring, the stones in a seki are alive, so each player counts their own seki stones. The shared liberties touch both colours, so, like any neutral point, they count for nobody.

Problems

Part 8

Play a full game

Play a complete 9×9 game against the computer, and this time check the life of every group as you go. Some advice before you start:

  • Check your groups before you play elsewhere. When one of your groups is surrounded, count its eye space and look at its shape. If the shape is unsettled, take the vital point now, before your opponent does.
  • Don’t add moves to groups that are already alive. A straight four, a bent four on the side, or a group with two eyes that are miai needs no more stones. Play the biggest move somewhere else.
  • Look at the diagonal points of your eyes. If your opponent can take one on the edge, or two in the centre, your eye may be false.
  • When you attack, squeeze first. Use hane from the outside to make the space smaller, then play the placement on the vital point.
  • Leave a seki alone. If two groups share their last liberties and neither has two eyes, don’t fill those liberties. Pass when only they are left.

Summary

What to remember

  1. The eye space of a group is all the empty points inside it. Its size and its shape decide whether the group can make two eyes.
  2. Straight four and bent four on the side are alive. Square four is dead, even if the owner plays first.
  3. Straight three, bent three, pyramid four, bulky five and cross five are unsettled: whoever takes the vital point first decides. The flower six dies if the attacker takes its centre first.
  4. The vital point is usually the point of the space that touches the most other points.
  5. An eye is false if the opponent holds one of its diagonal points on the edge, or two of them in the centre.
  6. To kill a big group, make its space smaller from the outside with hane first, then play the placement.
  7. In a seki, neither side can fill a shared liberty without losing its own group. Leave it alone. Seki stones count for their owner, and shared liberties count for nobody.

Words from this lesson

Eye space
All the empty points inside a surrounded group, where it might make its eyes.
Unsettled
A group that lives if its owner plays first and dies if the opponent plays first.
Miai
Two points that work as a pair: if your opponent takes one, you take the other and are still fine.
False eye
An eye that the opponent can force you to fill, because the stones around it are not safely connected.
Hane
A move that wraps around the end of the opponent’s line of stones, touching it and sitting diagonally next to one of your own stones.
Placement
A stone played inside the opponent’s eye space, usually on its vital point.
Seki
Two groups that share liberties so that neither side can attack: both stay alive, and the shared liberties count for nobody.
