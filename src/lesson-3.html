Lesson 3 of 8

Suicide and Ko

You now know almost every rule of Go. Two are left, and both look strange the first time you meet them. This lesson explains what they say, why the game needs them, and the clever tactics that grow out of them.

  • Know when a move with no liberties is illegal, and when it is allowed.
  • See why a point surrounded by your own stones is safe from your opponent.
  • Set up a snapback: give away one stone to capture several.
  • Fight a ko using ko threats.

Part 1

The suicide rule

You are not allowed to place a stone where it would have no liberties at all. Such a move is called suicide, and it is illegal. If it were allowed, the stone would be captured the instant it landed, so the move would change nothing except to waste a turn.

On the board below you always play Black. White has left two empty points that are completely surrounded by white stones: D4 in the middle, and A7 in the top left corner. Try to play Black on each of them and read the message you get. Then play anywhere else to see that normal moves still work.

A detail about rule sets

A few rule sets (New Zealand and Ing rules) allow suicide of a whole chain of two or more stones. It is almost never a good move, so in practice everyone plays as if suicide is always illegal. These lessons use that rule.

Part 2

The exception: a move that captures is always allowed

Sometimes a point looks surrounded, but playing there captures one or more of the surrounding stones. That move is legal. The order of events matters:

  1. Place your stone.
  2. Remove every enemy chain that now has no liberties.
  3. Only then check whether your own stone has any liberties.

Because the captured stones come off the board first, the points where they stood become liberties for your new stone. So the move is not suicide.

Your turn: capture from the inside

In each problem you are Black. The white chain has exactly one liberty left, and that liberty is a point inside White’s own shape. Find it and capture.

Part 3

A first look at eyes

The suicide rule has a big consequence. An empty point that is surrounded only by your own stones is called an eye. Your opponent cannot play in your eye, because a stone there would have no liberties, unless that stone captures your whole chain.

Step through the example. The white chain is a ring of eight stones around the empty point D4. It has twelve liberties on the outside and one more, D4, on the inside.

Hold on to this question

Black could capture the ring only because the eye was the last liberty, and Black could fill it with a single move. What if the white chain had two separate eyes? Black could never play in either one, because each move would be suicide while the other eye was still empty. A chain like that can never be captured. That is the key idea of Lesson 4.

Part 4

Snapback: give one stone, take five

Here is a tactic that comes straight out of the capture rule. You deliberately place a stone where your opponent can capture it. But capturing it fills one of their own liberties, and leaves their chain with a single liberty: the point where your stone just was. You play there again and capture the whole chain. This is called a snapback.

Step through the example. Watch the four white stones in the bottom left corner. They have two liberties, D1 and D2.

If you play chess

A snapback is a sacrifice, the same idea as giving up a piece to open a mating attack. The sacrificed stone is a bait. Before you accept any free stone in Go, check what your own chain’s liberties will be after you take it.

Your turn: find the snapback

Part 5

Ko: the rule against going in circles

Look at the shape below. Black can capture the white stone at C4 by playing D4. But then the new black stone at D4 has only one liberty, at C4. So White could capture it straight back by playing C4, and the board would look exactly as it did before Black’s capture. Then Black could capture again, and so on forever.

This shape is called a ko (劫), and the ko rule stops the loop:

The ko rule

If a single stone has just captured a single stone, and capturing straight back would repeat the position, the opponent may not recapture on their very next move. They must play somewhere else first. After that, recapturing is allowed again.

If you play chess

Chess solves the same problem differently. If a position repeats three times, the game can be declared a draw. Go doesn’t allow draws by repetition, so instead it simply forbids the repeating move, which forces the players to fight the ko somewhere else on the board.

Ko threats

So what does the player who can’t recapture do? They play a ko threat: a move somewhere else that threatens something big, so big that the opponent must answer it. After the opponent answers, the ko is legal to retake. Now it is the other player’s turn to find a threat. A ko fight is this back-and-forth, and it ends when one side runs out of threats worth answering, or decides the ko isn’t worth it and ends it by filling the point.

Step through a full cycle below.

If you play Civilization or Polytopia

A ko fight is a trade. Each time your opponent ignores your threat to win the ko, they let you take something elsewhere. Each time they answer it, they give you back the ko. The real question is always: is the ko worth more than what I am giving up for it? Sometimes the best move is to let the ko go and take the compensation.

Try the ko rule yourself

This board has the same ko in the middle and two black stones in the top right corner. Black and White take turns. Follow these four steps:

  1. As Black, capture at D4.
  2. As White, try to take back immediately at C4. Read the message: the move is refused.
  3. As White, play the ko threat at F6 instead, which puts the two black corner stones in atari. As Black, answer it at G5.
  4. As White, now retake the ko at C4. This time it is allowed.

The exercise is complete when White retakes the ko legally. If you make a mistake, press Undo or Reset.

Your turn: fight the ko

Two questions and a problem. The problem is the hardest thing in this lesson, so take your time.

Part 6

Play: first to capture two

This is Capture Go again, with a longer target: the first player to capture two stones wins. Because one capture no longer ends the game, snapbacks and kos can now happen. Watch for them on both sides:

  • Before you capture a single stone, check how many liberties your capturing stone will have. If it will have only one, you may be walking into a snapback or a ko.
  • If the computer takes a ko, remember that you can’t retake straight away. Play a threat first.

Win one game to complete the lesson.

Summary

What to remember

  1. You may not play a stone that would have no liberties (suicide), unless the move captures something.
  2. When you place a stone, captured enemy stones are removed first. Only then is your own stone checked for liberties.
  3. An eye is an empty point surrounded by one player’s stones. The opponent can only play there if the move captures.
  4. In a snapback, your opponent captures one of your stones and is left in atari, so you capture back more.
  5. In a ko, you may not recapture straight away. Play a ko threat elsewhere first, and retake after your opponent answers.

Words from this lesson

Suicide
Placing a stone where it would have no liberties without capturing anything. It is illegal.
Eye
An empty point surrounded by one player’s stones, where the opponent can’t play unless the move captures.
Snapback
A sacrifice: your opponent captures your stone, which leaves their own chain in atari, and you capture it.
Ko
A shape where two single stones could capture each other back and forth forever. Immediate recapture is forbidden.
Ko threat
A move elsewhere that threatens something big, played so that you may retake the ko after your opponent answers it.
