Lesson 2 of 4

Ladders and Nets

In Lesson 1 you captured stones that were already in trouble. Now you will learn to hunt a stone that is trying to escape. This is where calculation comes in: before you start a chase, you work out how it ends, the same way you would check a forcing line in chess.

  • Choose the right side to put a stone in atari.
  • Read a ladder to the end before you play it.
  • Spot a ladder breaker and know when a ladder fails.
  • Catch a stone with a net, and win stones with a double atari.

Part 1

Two liberties: which side do you push from?

A stone with two liberties can be put in atari in two different ways: you can fill either of the two liberties. The stone will then run out through the other one. So the real question is not “can I put it in atari?” but “where do I want it to run?”

The rule of thumb is simple: atari from the side that drives the stone toward the edge of the board or toward your own stones. Those are the places where it will find fewer liberties. If you drive it into open space, it gains liberties and gets away.

If you play chess

This is like choosing which check to give. Two checks can look equally aggressive, but one drives the king into the open and the other drives it into a mating net. The good player asks where the king will go next, not just whether it is in check.

Your turn: choose the right atari

You are Black. In each problem, find the move that leads to capturing the white stone. If White runs, the computer will answer for White and you keep going until the stone is captured.

Part 2

The ladder

Sometimes you can keep a stone in atari move after move. Each time it runs, it gets exactly two liberties, and you immediately take one of them away again. The chase zigzags across the board like a staircase until the running stones hit the edge and die. This pattern is called a ladder (征 shicho).

Step through the example, or press Play to watch it run. The green dots show White’s liberties after every move. Notice that White never has more than two.

A Go proverb

“If you don’t know ladders, don’t play Go.” Ladders come up in almost every game. You need to be able to look at a stone with two liberties and know, without playing a single move, whether a ladder would catch it.

In a real game, White would never run all the way to the edge like that. A strong player reads the ladder in their head, sees that it fails, and gives up the stone right away. Each extra running stone only makes the loss bigger.

Now run the ladder yourself. The computer will play White and always choose the most stubborn escape.

Part 3

The ladder breaker

A ladder only works if the path is clear. If a white stone is already sitting somewhere along the path, the running chain will reach it and connect. Suddenly it has three or four liberties, and the chase is over. That waiting stone is called a ladder breaker.

Here is the same ladder as before, with one difference: there is a white stone at D4.

Why a failed ladder is a disaster

When a ladder works, you capture a whole chain. When it fails, you are left with a long diagonal line of single stones, each with only two or three liberties, and the opponent gets to pick them off with double ataris. A failed ladder usually loses the game on a small board. That is why you read the whole ladder before you play the first move, not while you are playing it.

Part 4

The net

When a ladder doesn’t work, there is sometimes a quieter way to catch the stone. Instead of putting it in atari, you play one point further away, so that the stone is loosely surrounded. Whichever way it tries to run, it never gets more than two liberties, and your stones are already waiting to finish the job. This is called a net (下駄 geta).

In the example below, the four white stones near the corners break every ladder on the board, so putting the stone at E5 in atari would let it escape. Watch what the net does instead.

If you play chess

A net is a quiet move, like a chess move that doesn’t give check but leaves the king with no safe squares. Beginners look only at ataris, the same way beginners in chess look only at checks. Always ask whether a quiet move would be stronger.

Your turn: cast the net

You are Black. In both problems, the corner stones break the ladders, so an atari lets White run away. Find the net. If White tries to run, keep going until the stone is captured.

Part 5

Double atari

A double atari is one move that puts two separate enemy chains in atari at the same time. Your opponent can save only one of them, so you capture the other. It is the Go version of a fork in chess, where one knight move attacks two pieces at once.

To find a double atari, look for an empty point that is a liberty of two different enemy chains, each of which has only two liberties.

Part 6

Play: first to three captures

This game is played on the full 9×9 board, and the goal is now harder: the first player to capture three stones wins. A single capture is no longer enough, so you have to think about the whole board, not just the nearest fight.

Use what you learned in this lesson:

  • Before you start a ladder, follow the zigzag path with your eyes all the way to the edge. If a computer stone is in the way, don’t start it.
  • If one of your stones gets caught in a ladder that works, stop running. Every extra stone you add will be captured too.
  • When a single atari won’t work, look for a net.
  • Look for points that are a liberty of two weak computer chains at once. That is where a double atari lives.

Summary

What to remember

  1. When you put a stone with two liberties in atari, choose the side that drives it toward the edge or toward your own stones.
  2. A ladder keeps a chain in atari on every move until it reaches the edge and is captured. Read it all the way to the end before you start it.
  3. A stone sitting on the ladder’s path is a ladder breaker. So is a stone that threatens the chasing stones. Either one makes the ladder fail.
  4. A failed ladder leaves you with a line of weak stones that your opponent can pick off with double ataris.
  5. When atari doesn’t work, a net can: a quiet move one point away that leaves the stone no way out.
  6. A double atari threatens two chains at once, and your opponent can save only one.

Words from this lesson

Reading
Working out a sequence of moves in your head before you play them, the way you calculate a forcing line in chess.
Ladder
A chase in which a chain is put in atari on every move and zigzags across the board until it is captured at the edge.
Ladder breaker
A stone that stops a ladder, usually by sitting on its path so the running chain can connect to it and gain liberties.
Net
A move that loosely surrounds a stone without putting it in atari, so that every way it tries to run fails.
Double atari
One move that puts two separate chains in atari at once. The opponent can save only one of them.
